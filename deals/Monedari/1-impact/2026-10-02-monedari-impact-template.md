## The problem and why it matters

**Small shops sell the daily basket, mostly for cash.** In Latin America the neighborhood store is where households buy everyday goods. In Colombia 453,750 such stores hold 37% of mass-consumption sales, and Colombia and Mexico hold 13.1% and 24.9% of the region's neighborhood stores [1]. In Peru they hold 72% of the retail market [2]. Yet at the counter, payment in Mexico and Colombia is still mostly cash. A shop that cannot accept a card or a transfer can lose the sale to a convenience chain down the street, which is the loss Monedari was built to stop.

**The pattern repeats across three countries.** The table sets the same three indicators side by side.

| Country | Neighborhood stores | How people pay at small shops | Account access or cash preference |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mexico | 24.9% of the region's neighborhood stores (NielsenIQ, reported August 2026) [1] | 85% of purchases under MXN 500 paid in cash, 10.4% by card, 4.4% by transfer, at physical points of sale (2024) [3] | 63.0% of adults aged 18 to 70 hold a formal savings account (2024) [4] |
| Colombia | 453,750 stores, 13.1% of the region's, 37% of mass-consumption sales (NielsenIQ, reported August 2026) [1] | Cash is 77.8% of payments by number and 78.6% by value (survey fieldwork late 2023) [5] | 95.8% of adults hold a deposit product (2024) [6] |
| Peru | 72% of the retail market (2023) [2] | 73.3% of sales in Lima's bodegas go through digital wallets, 25% in cash (2026) [7] | 65% of Peruvians still prefer to pay in cash (reported January 2025) [2] |

**Peru shows what a free wallet can do.** Where a free wallet reached the counter, most of Lima's bodega sales now move digitally [7]. Mexico and Colombia have not reached that point, and the cash share at the counter is the room Monedari works in.

**Providers do not go to the shop.** In Mexico, 55.57% of shopkeepers had no card terminal in the second half of 2024. The trade association ANPEC reports that owners distrust banks, feel intimidated by them, fear closer tax scrutiny of electronic sales, and deal with suppliers and customers who work in cash [8]. A conventional terminal also brings a rental or upfront cost, a contract and days of waiting for the money.

**Women owners and falling shop sales add pressure.** Women hold formal savings accounts less often than men: 58.6% against 68.0% in Mexico (2024) [3], and in Colombia women trail men by 6.9 points in access to financial products (2024) [6]. The shops themselves are under strain. ANPEC reports a 15% fall in sales and more than 60,000 Mexican shops at risk of closing, linked to the 2026 tax increase on soft drinks and cigarettes [9].

**The timing favors acceptance on a phone.** Card payments in Mexico grew 14% in 2025 to 11,261 million. A Visa study reported by Expansión finds that the share of people using contactless payments rose from 2% to 30% in about two years [10]. Tap-to-phone needs no terminal, which removes the rental and the contract.

**Regulation separates accepting payments from holding money.** In Mexico, platforms that hold clients' funds as electronic payment institutions need authorization from the banking and securities regulator (CNBV) under the 2018 Fintech Law [11]. In Colombia, acquirers can be credit institutions, specialized electronic deposit and payment companies, or unsupervised companies registered for the purpose, and aggregators act for an acquirer under contract and need not be financial entities [12]. Monedari pays shops into their own bank accounts and processes cards through its acquiring bank under an aggregator agreement, rather than holding balances for them. Card acceptance in Colombia will need a local acquirer. **PENDING:** confirmation of the licenses Monedari holds or needs in each country, including for its working-capital advances.

## Relevance of Monedari to the problem

**Monedari turns the shop owner's phone into a terminal.** A shop owner's Android phone becomes a card terminal: customers tap a card or phone on it. QR payments by bank transfer are free to the shop. The money lands in the shop's bank account the next business day, and every sale, cash included, goes into a daily sales ledger. There is no hardware, and a route rep or Monedari agent gets a shop live in about 10 minutes, with the first sale on the same visit. That takes away the rental, the contract and the wait.

**Each side gets something it lacked.** Shop owners accept cards without a terminal, are paid the next day and see their sales in one place. They can add Monedari Plus (about USD 6.50 a month, for inventory, supplier orders and weekly sales reports) and working-capital advances through Adelanto, a pilot with 640 advances so far, repaid as a share of daily card sales. Customers pay the way they already want to, by card, phone or transfer. The scale today is 14,200 active merchants moving USD 9.8M a month, 62% by card and 38% by QR.

## Business model

**Shops pay when they sell, and pay for extras.** Monedari earns on three lines from the same app. On card payments the shop pays 3.2% plus IVA, and Monedari keeps a net 1.17% of card volume after card network and acquirer costs. Monedari Plus is a subscription of about USD 6.50 a month, with 2,600 subscribers. Adelanto earns a fixed fee per advance. QR payments are free to the shop: they bring shops in and keep them active. Shops are found through two regional consumer-goods wholesalers, whose route reps already visit thousands of shops every week and now sign them up, and through Monedari's own field force. The wholesalers brought 46% of new merchants in 2026.

**One average shop shows the economics.** The average active shop brings Monedari about USD 7.04 a month in net revenue and, at a 54% gross margin, about USD 3.80 a month in gross profit (calculated from the figures above). Signing a shop costs USD 24 through a wholesaler and USD 58 through a field agent. At that gross profit, a wholesaler-sourced shop repays its acquisition cost in about 6 months and a field-sourced shop in about 15 (calculated).

**Three risks, each with its management.**

- **One acquiring bank.** All card processing runs through a single acquiring bank. Part of this round funds integration of a second acquirer.
- **Larger providers copy the feature.** Banks and larger payment companies now offer card acceptance on a phone as well [10]. Monedari competes where they do not go: wholesaler route reps reach shops in person, and the ledger, supplier tools and working capital keep shops on the app beyond card acceptance.
- **Lending to shops.** Adelanto is a pilot funded from Monedari's own balance sheet, repaid as a share of daily card sales so that repayment follows the shop's takings. It scales only as underwriting is proven, and the round funds an in-house risk engine and dedicated lending capital.

## Alignment with the fund's impact lens

**Shops that ran on cash now take digital payments and keep a record.** 61% of its merchants had never accepted a digital payment before Monedari, and 58% of its shops are run by women (company-reported, from its onboarding survey). For these owners the change is a sale they no longer lose, next-day money and a written record of what they sell. Revenue and outcome move together: Monedari earns its main revenue only when a shop accepts a card.

| Dimension | Change driven by Monedari |
|---|---|
| What | Shops accept card and transfer payments, many for the first time, and get next-day money and a record of every sale, cash included. The outcome is productive: sales kept, and a record on which stock and working capital can be based. |
| Who | Owners of neighborhood shops that banks do not visit, with a majority of women owners. They need a bank account to be paid out, so the shops reached are banked: about 37% of Mexican adults hold no formal account and are not reached today [4]. |
| How much | Depth and durability: shops from 2026 cohorts stay on the platform longer than 2025 cohorts did, with six-month retention of 71% against 62% (company-reported). Reach is stated in the key data above. |
| Contribution | Without Monedari, a shop chooses between cash, a rented terminal and a bank terminal that needs a branch visit. Larger providers now offer phone acceptance, so for some shops the alternative is another provider rather than cash. Monedari's contribution is greatest in shops that route reps visit in person and that online sign-up would not reach. |
| Risk | Impact shrinks if shops drop off after sign-up, if free transfers make card acceptance unnecessary, or if advances overextend a shop. Monedari manages this through retention tracked by cohort, free QR that keeps shops engaged, and an advances pilot repaid from sales. Shops are answered by Monedari's own support team on WhatsApp, in Spanish, seven days a week, and money goes straight to the owner's bank account. |

**One gap in the fit.** The outcome is first-time digital acceptance and a sales record for already-banked shop owners, not entry into the banking system. These owners are poorly served by banks for payments and credit, though they hold an account. Reaching owners with no account would need a payout account of Monedari's own, which it does not offer today.

| Impact outcome | KPIs to track |
|---|---|
| Shops accepting digital payments for the first time | Share of active merchants that had never accepted a digital payment before joining; baseline 61% (company-reported) |
| Women-owned shops reached | Share and number of active merchants run by women; baseline 58% (company-reported, from onboarding survey) |
| Shops that keep using digital acceptance | Share of merchants still accepting digital payments six months after joining, by cohort; baseline 71% for 2026 cohorts, 62% for 2025 cohorts (company-reported) |

**What the KPIs will track.** The baselines marked company-reported come from Monedari's own onboarding and transaction data. The first quarterly report will confirm them from that data and set the baseline for the last KPI.

## Strategy to scale impact after the investment

**The round funds expansion and the machinery behind it.** Of the USD 3M seed round, 45% goes to four more Mexican states, 25% to a second acquirer integration and an in-house risk engine, 20% to Colombia and 10% to lending capital for Adelanto. The lead and the angels are already in, so the round adds to a funded plan rather than starting one.

**The two-year targets are the company's own.** Net revenue is USD 1.2M annualized today. Monedari's targets are USD 3.4M of net revenue in 2027 and USD 9.5M in 2028, with 42,000 and 85,000 active merchants at the end of each year.

**Each new shop is cheaper to add.** A wholesaler route rep or a Monedari agent visits, the owner downloads the app, takes an ID photo and adds a bank account, and the first sale happens on the same visit. Wholesaler routes already pass thousands of shops every week at less than half the cost of a field agent (see the business model), so the average cost of adding a shop falls as the wholesaler share grows.

**Where it grows next.**

- **More Mexican states.** Risk: acquisition costs can rise outside the current cluster. Mitigation: the pairing of wholesaler routes and own field force already runs in four states, and the round pays for repeating it.
- **Card acceptance in Colombia.** Risk: cards need a local acquirer. Mitigation: QR is already live in Bogota, so shops are onboarded and card acceptance follows once an acquirer is in place.
- **Working capital through Adelanto.** Risk: advances could overextend shops if underwriting slips. Mitigation: it stays a pilot repaid as a share of card sales while the risk engine and lending capital are built.
